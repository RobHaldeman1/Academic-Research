Arrests, Revenue, and Returns: A Multi-Measure Analysis of Macroeconomic Drivers in U.S. Sports Betting

Examining whether modern sports gambling functions as a pro-cyclical normal good driven by economic prosperity—rather than financial desperation.

Robert J. Haldeman March 2026 2015–2025 Analysis OLS · CAPM · Time-Series
Section I

Abstract

Summary

The rapid expansion of the U.S. sports betting market raises a critical question for economic modeling and regulatory policy: does consumer gambling behavior reflect financial distress or discretionary wealth? This study examines whether modern sports gambling functions as a pro-cyclical normal good driven by economic prosperity rather than financial desperation. Utilizing a 120-month time-series dataset (2015–2025) of U.S. gambling arrests and macroeconomic indicators, this research employs Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regressions to evaluate the relationship between macroeconomic conditions and gambling activity.

The baseline specifications strongly support the thesis: the Unemployment Rate remains statistically insignificant, while excess capital metrics like Job Openings (JOLTS) and Real Disposable Personal Income demonstrate a highly significant, positive correlation with gambling arrests. A supplementary analysis using monthly legal sports betting revenue corroborates these findings. A Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) analysis of Penn Entertainment suggests that financial markets price the retail gambling sector as highly cyclical. Ultimately, these findings confirm that the sports betting industry is fueled by robust macroeconomic health and excess consumer capital.

Section III

Introduction

The rapid expansion of the United States sports betting market following the 2018 Supreme Court repeal of the Professional and Amateur Sports Protection Act (PASPA) has fundamentally transformed the accessibility and cultural presence of gambling. As this multi-billion-dollar industry transitions from underground, illicit markets to mainstream mobile platforms, a critical macroeconomic question emerges: what underlying economic conditions drive consumer participation in sports gambling?

Historically, conventional assumptions and select behavioral theories have occasionally framed illicit gambling as a counter-cyclical coping mechanism—a desperate attempt to generate alternative income during periods of high unemployment and financial distress. However, assessing the actual macroeconomic and financial market data reveals a vastly different reality. This paper argues that sports gambling does not act as a financial refuge during times of economic hardship, but rather functions as a pro-cyclical, discretionary entertainment good that expands alongside consumer wealth, job security, and overall macroeconomic prosperity.

To empirically test this thesis, this study conducts a comprehensive analysis spanning a 120-month period from September 2015 to August 2025, capturing both the pre-legalization “illegal era” and the subsequent legal sports betting boom. The primary analysis employs OLS regressions to examine the relationship between illegal gambling arrest rates and key macroeconomic indicators. By testing desperation metrics (Unemployment Rate, CPI) against prosperity metrics (JOLTS, Real Disposable Personal Income), this paper quantifies the true drivers of consumer betting volume.

Section IV

Methodology

To empirically test the thesis that sports gambling functions as a pro-cyclical, discretionary good fueled by economic prosperity, this study employs a multi-faceted analytical approach centered on OLS regression analysis, supplemented by a revenue cross-validation and a CAPM observation.

5.1   Macroeconomic OLS Regression

The first phase uses time-series data on 4,765 gambling-related arrests in the U.S. to determine how macroeconomic indicators of labor market strength and excess capital impact illicit gambling volume. Four model specifications are estimated based on the baseline equation:

Arrestsit = β0 + β1 · UnemploymentRateit + γXit + εit

Where Xt represents a set of controls including CPI, average hourly earnings, JOLTS, and labor force statistics.

5.2   Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)

As a supplementary observation, this study examines the systematic risk (β) of Penn Entertainment (PENN). Using monthly financial data spanning the legal sports betting era (June 2018 to August 2025), the analysis compares PENN’s asset returns against broader market performance:

Section V

Empirical Results

Across the four estimated baseline specifications, the coefficient for the Unemployment Rate showed changes in both direction and statistical significance. Initial findings showed a coefficient of −2.61, statistically significant at the 5% level. However, as control variables were added the relationship weakened. In the final baseline (specification 4) it was −0.09 and statistically insignificant.

The shift from an initially significant estimate to an insignificant one suggests the earlier results suffered from omitted variable bias. Once the full set of controls is included, Job Openings (JOLTS) remains highly significant and positive at the 1% level (+5.08), indicating a strong and persistent relationship between labor demand and gambling-related arrests.

Robustness Checks

Five alternative specifications tested stability against data anomalies, omitted variable bias, and time-series violations—including trimmed outliers, first-differencing, and AR(1) corrections. Across all checks, the Unemployment Rate remained statistically insignificant, while JOLTS and Real Disposable Personal Income proved highly significant.

Table 3. Alternative Specifications—Robustness checks including trimmed outliers, Real Disposable Personal Income, first-differencing, and AR(1) correction. The Unemployment Rate remains insignificant across every specification.

Revenue Cross-Validation

Using total monthly U.S. sports betting revenue (85 months, July 2018–July 2025), the revenue model reveals a direct, negative relationship between economic hardship and gambling expenditure. A one-percentage-point increase in the Unemployment Rate is associated with an approximate $38.86 million decline in monthly sports betting revenue, holding all other factors constant.

Section VI

Conclusion

Across both the baseline models and rigorous alternative specifications, the empirical evidence consistently rejects the hypothesis that economic hardship drives sports gambling. The Unemployment Rate remained statistically insignificant across every single specification. Instead, the data reveals that gambling arrests are overwhelmingly driven by metrics of economic strength.

Variables representing excess capital and labor market stability—specifically Job Openings (JOLTS) and Real Disposable Personal Income—proved highly significant and positively correlated with increased gambling activity, even after correcting for time-series non-stationarity and autocorrelation.

The consistently negative unemployment coefficient across both the arrest and revenue models provides convergent evidence that sports gambling does not function as a desperation-driven activity. Whether measured through illegal market participation or legal consumer spending, gambling volume contracts rather than expands during periods of economic hardship.

Policymakers should recognize that gambling tax revenues are likely to contract during economic downturns precisely because sports betting behaves as discretionary spending. States that have come to depend on gambling tax receipts should therefore build fiscal buffers to account for this pro-cyclical volatility.
